4:T517,Sound is vibration. When something vibrates, it creates waves that travel through the air to your ears. Think of dropping a stone in water—the ripples spread outward. Sound works the same way, except the vibrations are invisible.

Music is organised sound. Instead of random vibrations, we use specific vibrations that our brains find pleasing. These vibrations happen at different speeds, which we call frequencies. A fast vibration creates a high sound (like a whistle), while a slow vibration creates a low sound (like a drum).

Every musical note is a specific frequency. When you hear a piano play middle C, that's a vibration happening 262 times per second. An octave higher (the next C up) vibrates twice as fast—524 times per second. This relationship between frequencies is what makes music work.

Instruments create these vibrations in different ways. A guitar string vibrates when plucked. A drum head vibrates when struck. Your vocal cords vibrate when you sing. All of these create the frequencies we hear as music.

The key thing to understand is that music isn't magic—it's physics. Once you know how sound works, understanding music becomes much easier. Everything in music theory builds on this simple idea: organised vibrations at specific frequencies create the sounds we enjoy.5:T547,A note is a single sound at a specific pitch. Think of notes like letters in the alphabet—they're the building blocks of music. In Western music, we have 12 basic notes that repeat in different octaves (higher or lower versions).

The 12 notes are: C, C#, D, D#, E, F, F#, G, G#, A, A#, B. The # symbol means 'sharp' (slightly higher). These 12 notes repeat forever—after B comes C again, just higher.

A scale is a selection of notes in a specific order. The most common scale is the major scale, which has 8 notes (the 8th note is the same as the 1st, just higher). If you start on C and play every white key on a piano, you're playing a C major scale: C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C.

A key is the home base of a piece of music. If a song is 'in the key of C major', it means the song revolves around C and uses the notes from the C major scale. Think of it like a home address—C is home, and the other notes in the scale are nearby.

Different scales create different feelings. Major scales sound happy and bright. Minor scales sound sad or mysterious. This is why composers choose specific keys for their songs.

Understanding scales and keys helps you predict which notes will sound good together. If you know a song is in G major, you know which notes to use without thinking about it. This is incredibly useful for songwriting and improvisation.6:T571,Rhythm is the pattern of sounds and silences in music. It's the 'when' of music—when notes happen. Without rhythm, music would just be a collection of random notes. Rhythm is what makes you want to tap your foot.

Tempo is the speed of the music, measured in beats per minute (BPM). A slow song might be 60 BPM (one beat per second), while a fast dance song might be 120 BPM (two beats per second). Tempo is like the heartbeat of the music.

A time signature tells you how many beats are in each bar (or measure) and what type of note gets one beat. The most common time signature is 4/4, which means 'four beats per bar, and a quarter note gets one beat'. You count: 1, 2, 3, 4, 1, 2, 3, 4.

Think of time signatures like containers. 4/4 is a container that holds exactly four beats. 3/4 (used in waltzes) holds three beats. 6/8 holds six beats. Different time signatures create different feels—3/4 feels like a waltz, while 4/4 feels like a march.

Within each bar, you can divide the beats into smaller notes. A quarter note is one beat. An eighth note is half a beat. A half note is two beats. A whole note is four beats. This is how you create interesting rhythmic patterns.

Rhythm is what separates music from just playing notes. Two songs can use the same notes but sound completely different because of their rhythm and tempo. Mastering rhythm is essential to understanding music.7:T5b5,A chord is a group of notes played together. When notes are played simultaneously, they create harmony. Some combinations of notes sound pleasant (consonant), while others sound harsh or tense (dissonant).

The most basic chord is a triad, which has three notes. A major triad is built by taking a note (the root), skipping one note, taking the next note, skipping one, and taking the next note. For example, C major is C, E, G. These three notes sound happy and stable together.

A minor triad is similar, but the middle note is lowered. C minor is C, Eb, G. This sounds sad or dark compared to C major. The difference is just one note, but it completely changes the feeling.

Harmony works because of frequency ratios. When two notes have frequencies in a simple ratio (like 2:1 or 3:2), they sound good together. This is why certain chords work and others don't—it's based on maths, not opinion.

Chords are the foundation of most songs. A typical song might use three or four chords that repeat throughout. For example, many pop songs use C major, F major, and G major. These three chords work together because they share common notes and create a pleasing harmonic progression.

Understanding chords helps you understand why songs sound the way they do. When you hear a sad song, it's probably using minor chords. When you hear a happy song, it's probably using major chords. Understanding structure helps you write better songs and appreciate why songs are arranged the way they are.

The verse is where the story happens. Verses usually have different lyrics each time they appear and tell the narrative of the song. Think of verses like paragraphs in a book—each one adds new information. Most songs have two to four verses.

The chorus is the memorable, repeating part. The chorus has the same lyrics and melody every time it appears. It's usually the catchiest part of the song—the part people remember and sing along to. The chorus typically appears after each verse and reinforces the main message.

The bridge is a contrasting section that appears once, usually near the end. It breaks up the repetition of verses and choruses by introducing something different—different melody, different lyrics, different feel. After the bridge, the song usually returns to the chorus, which feels satisfying because you've heard something new.

A typical pop song structure is: Verse, Chorus, Verse, Chorus, Bridge, Chorus. Some songs add an intro (instrumental at the start) and an outro (ending section). Some songs have pre-choruses (a short section before the chorus that builds anticipation).

Different genres use different structures. Hip-hop might have longer verses with shorter choruses. Country songs might have longer storytelling sections. Electronic dance music might repeat the same section many times.

Understanding structure helps you write songs that feel complete and satisfying. It shows you exactly what notes to play, when to play them, and how long to play them. Learning to read sheet music opens up the entire world of written music.

The foundation is the staff, which has five horizontal lines. Notes sit on the lines or in the spaces between them. The higher a note sits on the staff, the higher the pitch. The lower it sits, the lower the pitch.

At the beginning of the staff is a clef symbol. The treble clef (looks like a fancy G) is used for higher instruments like flute, violin, and right hand of piano. The bass clef (looks like a backwards C) is used for lower instruments like cello, tuba, and left hand of piano. The clef tells you which notes are which.

Note shapes tell you duration. A whole note (hollow oval) lasts four beats. A half note (hollow oval with a stem) lasts two beats. A quarter note (filled oval with a stem) lasts one beat. An eighth note (filled oval with a stem and a flag) lasts half a beat. This is how rhythm is written.

Other symbols add information. A sharp (#) raises a note by one semitone. A flat (b) lowers it by one semitone. A natural (♮) cancels a sharp or flat. Rests (symbols that look like pauses) show silence.

The time signature appears at the start, after the clef. The key signature (sharps or flats) shows which key the piece is in. Dynamics (like 'p' for soft and 'f' for loud) tell you how loudly to play.

Reading sheet music takes practice, but it's like learning to read any language. Start slowly, learn the basics, and gradually build speed.
