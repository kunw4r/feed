4:T66d,Ever notice how some people can calculate tips instantly while others reach for their phone? The secret isn't being a maths genius—it's knowing a few clever shortcuts.

Let's start with the foundation: understanding place value. When you see 247, think of it as 200 + 40 + 7. This breaks big numbers into bite-sized chunks your brain can handle easily.

Here's a practical trick for multiplying by 11. Want to work out 23 × 11? Add the digits (2 + 3 = 5) and pop that answer between them: 253. Try 34 × 11: add 3 + 4 = 7, so the answer is 374. It works because you're essentially shifting the number left and adding the original.

For percentages, remember that 10% is just dividing by 10. Need 20% of $80? Find 10% first ($8), then double it ($16). This method works for any percentage—just build it from 10% chunks.

Rounding is your friend. If you're splitting a $47 bill three ways, round to $48 (easier to divide by 3 = $16 each). You're only off by a few cents, but your brain does the work instantly.

The casting out nines trick helps you check multiplication. Add the digits of each number until you get a single digit, multiply those, then check if the result matches your answer's digit sum. It's like a maths safety net.

Why does this matter? Mental maths builds number sense—that gut feeling for whether an answer is reasonable. When the checkout says your $15 item costs $45, your brain should immediately flag that something's wrong.

Practice these tricks with real situations: calculating change, working out how long a trip takes, figuring out recipe quantities. Your brain gets faster the more you use these patterns.5:T6f4,Percentages are everywhere in your wallet: interest rates, discounts, tax, salary increases. Understanding them means understanding your money.

Think of a percentage as a slice of 100. If something costs $100 and there's a 20% discount, you're removing 20 slices out of 100, leaving 80. So you pay $80. Simple.

Here's where it gets tricky: percentages aren't always about the same amount. A 10% pay rise on $50,000 is $5,000 extra. A 10% pay rise on $30,000 is only $3,000 extra. The percentage is the same, but the actual money differs. This matters when comparing offers.

Interest is percentage in action. If you borrow $1,000 at 5% interest per year, you owe an extra $50 that year. But compound interest—where you pay interest on the interest—grows faster. After two years at 5%, you'd owe about $1,102.50, not $1,100. That extra $2.50 came from interest on the first year's interest. Over decades, this compounds into serious money.

Sales are designed to confuse you. A "50% off" sign sounds amazing, but 50% off $20 is only $10 savings. Meanwhile, a "30% off" on a $100 item saves you $30—more actual money, even though the percentage is smaller.

Tax works backwards from what you see. If something costs $110 including 10% tax, the original price wasn't $100—it was about $100. You need to divide by 1.10, not subtract 10%.

The golden rule: always ask "50% of what?" A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease doesn't get you back to where you started. A $100 item increased by 20% becomes $120. Decrease that by 20% and you get $96. The second percentage applies to a bigger number.

Practical tip: convert percentages to decimals. 15% becomes 0.15. Multiply your amount by this decimal. Not because studies are bad, but because they're often presented in ways that twist the truth.

Here's the first red flag: missing context. A headline screams "Coffee consumption up 300%!" Sounds alarming, right? But if coffee consumption went from 1% of people to 3%, that's technically a 300% increase. The actual change is tiny, but the percentage sounds huge. Always ask: 300% of what number?

Sample size matters enormously. If a study surveyed 10 people and found 8 liked a product, that's 80%. But with only 10 people, this tells you almost nothing. Survey 10,000 people and 80% means something real. Smaller samples are more likely to show weird results just by chance.

Selection bias is sneaky. A survey asking "Do you love our product?" on the company's website will only reach happy customers. People who hate it won't visit. This skews results dramatically. Good studies try to reach random, representative groups.

Correlation isn't causation—this is crucial. Ice cream sales and drowning deaths both increase in summer. They're correlated. But ice cream doesn't cause drowning; warm weather causes both. News stories often miss this distinction, suggesting false connections.

Watch for cherry-picked data. If a company tested their product 20 times and only advertise the 3 times it worked best, they're lying with statistics. Reputable studies report all their results, even the disappointing ones.

The baseline matters. "Rare disease cases doubled!" sounds terrifying. But if it went from 2 cases to 4 cases in a population of millions, it's still incredibly rare. Doubling a tiny number is still tiny.

Finally, check who funded the research. A study funded by a chocolate company about chocolate's health benefits deserves extra scrutiny. Bias doesn't mean the research is wrong, but it's worth questioning.

Your defence: demand the actual numbers, not just percentages. Ask about sample size. Check who conducted the study. Look for peer review. Healthy scepticism isn't cynicism—it's smart thinking.7:T919,Probability is just a fancy word for "what are the chances?" Understanding it helps you make smarter decisions about money, health, and risk.

Start with the basics: probability ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). A coin flip is 0.5 (50% chance heads). Rolling a six on a die is about 0.167 (one in six). These numbers tell you how likely something is.

Here's a common mistake: thinking past results affect future chances. If a coin landed heads five times in a row, people think tails is "due." It's not. The coin has no memory. The next flip is still 50-50. This is called the gambler's fallacy, and it costs people real money.

Independent events don't affect each other. The probability of rain tomorrow doesn't change whether it rained today. But dependent events do connect. The probability of drawing two aces from a deck changes after you draw the first ace—there's now one fewer ace in the deck.

Risk is probability times consequence. Skipping a seatbelt has a small probability of causing an accident, but the consequence is severe, so the risk is high. Eating one extra biscuit has a tiny consequence even if you do it regularly, so the risk is low. This is why we wear seatbelts but don't panic about one biscuit.

Lotteries are a brilliant example of poor probability thinking. Your chance of winning Powerball is about 1 in 292 million. You're more likely to be struck by lightning. Yet people spend billions on tickets. The expected value (probability × prize) is negative—you lose money on average.

Insurance works the opposite way. You pay a small amount (premium) to protect against a large, unlikely loss. The insurance company calculates the probability of claims and prices accordingly. It's rational risk management.

Base rates matter. If a disease affects 1 in 10,000 people and a test is 99% accurate, a positive result doesn't mean you have a 99% chance of having it. You need to factor in how rare the disease is. Most positive results will be false positives.

Practical application: when facing a decision, estimate the probability of different outcomes and their consequences. Should you take that job with lower pay but better security? Area is how much flat space something covers. Imagine painting a wall. You need to know its area to buy enough paint. A wall that's 4 metres wide and 3 metres tall has an area of 4 × 3 = 12 square metres. Rectangles are straightforward: length times width.

Circles are trickier but follow a pattern. The area of a circle is π × radius². If your circular table has a radius of 1 metre, the area is about 3.14 square metres. Why radius squared? Because you're measuring in two dimensions—the radius extends in all directions from the centre.

Volume measures how much space something holds inside. A box 2 metres long, 1 metre wide, and 1 metre tall holds 2 × 1 × 1 = 2 cubic metres. This matters when moving house (how much fits in the truck?) or filling a pool (how many litres?).

Cylinders (like paint tins) use the circle formula for one end, then multiply by height. A tin with a 0.1 metre radius and 0.5 metres tall holds about 0.0157 cubic metres (or roughly 15.7 litres).

Distance and the Pythagorean theorem solve real problems. If you're walking across a rectangular park that's 3 km one way and 4 km the other, cutting diagonally is shorter. The diagonal distance is √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5 km. You save 2 km by going diagonal.

Angles matter too. A ladder leaning against a wall creates angles. If the angle is too shallow, the ladder slips. Too steep, and you can't reach high enough. The right angle depends on the ladder length and wall height—more geometry.

Scales and ratios use geometry. A map showing 1 cm = 1 km means distances on the map are 100,000 times smaller than reality. Blueprints work the same way. Understanding the ratio lets you convert between the model and the real thing.

Practical tip: when measuring for a project, always sketch it out. Draw rectangles for rooms, circles for round objects. Label dimensions. Learning to read them critically protects you from dodgy claims.

Bar charts compare amounts across categories. A chart showing sales by month makes trends obvious—you can spot which months performed best at a glance. The key is checking the axis labels. If the vertical axis starts at 90 instead of 0, small differences look huge. A 5% change becomes visually dramatic when the scale is compressed.

Line graphs show change over time. Stock prices, temperature trends, population growth—all work well as line graphs. The slope tells the story: steep lines mean rapid change, flat lines mean stability. But again, watch the scale. A graph with a compressed vertical axis exaggerates small changes.

Pie charts show parts of a whole. If a budget is 50% rent, 30% food, 20% other, a pie chart makes this clear. But pie charts are terrible for comparing similar values—is 28% really different from 32%? A bar chart would show this more clearly.

Scatter plots show relationships between two things. Plot house size against price, and you'll see if bigger houses cost more. If the dots form a clear line going up, there's a strong relationship. If they're scattered randomly, there's no relationship.

Histograms look like bar charts but show distribution. How many students scored 60-70%, 70-80%, etc.? This reveals whether results cluster around one score or spread widely. It's different from a bar chart because the categories are continuous (overlapping ranges).

Here's the critical skill: check the source and axis labels first. Who made this graph? Do they have a reason to mislead? What are the axes measuring? Are the scales fair or exaggerated?

Look for missing context. A graph showing "crime up 50%" might hide that crime was already falling and this is just a small bump. A graph showing "unemployment at lowest in 20 years" might hide that many people stopped looking for work.

Finally, ask what's not shown. A graph of average salaries hides whether everyone earns close to average or if a few high earners skew the average upward. A graph of website traffic hides whether visitors are actually buying anything.

Practice: find a graph in the news, identify the type, check the axes, and ask what story it's telling.
