10:T6d1,Public speaking is like learning to swim. At first, the water seems terrifying, but once you understand how it works, you realise you can float. Speaking in public is one of the most valuable skills you can develop because it amplifies your influence. Whether you're pitching an idea at work, presenting to clients, or sharing your expertise, your ability to speak well determines whether people listen and act.

The fear you feel is completely normal. Your brain treats public speaking like a physical threat, triggering the fight-or-flight response. Your heart races, palms sweat, and your mind goes blank. This happens because humans are wired to fear judgment from the group. Thousands of years ago, being rejected by your tribe meant death. Today, that instinct still fires up when we face an audience.

Here's the secret: the fear doesn't disappear, but it transforms. Professional speakers still get nervous before big presentations. The difference is they've learned that nervousness is just energy. That adrenaline sharpens your thinking and makes you more animated. Instead of fighting the feeling, you redirect it.

Start small. Speak up in team meetings. Join a local Toastmasters club where everyone's learning together. Record yourself speaking and watch it back—you'll realise you're far better than you think. Each time you speak, your brain updates its threat assessment. After five or ten presentations, your nervous system learns that public speaking isn't actually dangerous.

Remember: your audience wants you to succeed. They're not hoping you'll fail. They're sitting there hoping you'll give them something valuable. That shift in perspective—from "they're judging me" to "I'm here to help them"—changes everything.11:T6e0,Think of a speech like a journey. If you just wander around without a map, people get lost and forget where they've been. A well-structured speech is like a clear route from point A to point B, with landmarks along the way that stick in people's minds.

The classic structure is simple: tell them what you're going to say, say it, then tell them what you said. This isn't boring repetition—it's how human memory works. We need preview, content, and review to lock information into our brains.

Start with a hook. This is your opening sentence that grabs attention. It could be a surprising statistic, a question, a short story, or a bold statement. Don't start with "Hello, my name is..." That's like a movie starting with credits. Jump straight into something interesting. For example: "One in three Australians will experience anxiety in their lifetime. I was one of them." Now people are listening.

Next, give your roadmap. Tell people exactly what you'll cover: "Today I'm sharing three strategies that helped me manage my anxiety." This primes their brain to listen for three things.

Then deliver your main points. Each point should have a story, example, or evidence. Don't just list facts. Facts alone are forgettable. Stories stick. If you're talking about resilience, don't just say "resilience matters." Tell about the time you failed spectacularly and what you learned.

Use the rule of three. Three points are memorable. Two feels incomplete. Four feels like you're rambling. Three is the sweet spot.

Finish with a strong conclusion that circles back to your opening. Restate your main points, then end with a call to action or memorable final thought. Don't just fade away or say "thanks for listening." Leave them with something to remember.12:T759,Your body is speaking before your mouth opens. Research suggests that 55% of communication is body language, 38% is tone of voice, and only 7% is actual words. This doesn't mean words don't matter—it means your delivery carries enormous weight.

Start with your stance. Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, weight balanced. Avoid swaying, pacing nervously, or standing rigidly like a statue. You want to look grounded and confident. Imagine roots growing from your feet into the ground.

Your hands should be visible and purposeful. Keep them out of your pockets. Use gestures that match your words. If you're talking about growth, move your hand upward. If you're describing something small, use a smaller gesture. Avoid repetitive gestures like touching your hair or clicking a pen—these distract from your message.

Make eye contact with different people in the room. Don't stare at one person or look at your notes constantly. Spend 3-5 seconds with each person, moving around the room. This creates connection and makes people feel seen.

Now for your voice. Speak more slowly than feels natural. Nervous speakers rush. Slow speech gives your words weight and gives listeners time to absorb. Pause between ideas. Silence is powerful. It lets ideas land.

Vary your pitch and pace. A monotone voice puts people to sleep. Emphasise important words by saying them slightly louder or slower. If you're describing something exciting, let your voice get faster and higher. If you're being serious, lower your pitch and slow down.

Projection matters. Speak from your diaphragm, not your throat. Imagine your voice needs to reach the back wall. This isn't shouting—it's controlled volume that fills the space.

Practice these elements separately, then together. Record yourself. Watch the playback. You'll notice habits you weren't aware of and improvements you can make.13:T772,Imagine you're at a party where someone talks at you for ten minutes straight without asking a question or checking if you're interested. That's boring. Now imagine someone who asks your opinion, listens to your answer, and builds on what you say. That's engaging. The same principle applies to public speaking.

Engagement starts before you speak. Know your audience. Who are they? What do they care about? What problems are they facing? Tailor your examples and language to them. If you're speaking to teenagers, don't use references from the 1980s. If you're speaking to accountants, use relevant examples from their field.

Use rhetorical questions. "Have you ever felt completely overwhelmed?" This isn't a question that needs an answer—it's a question that makes people think. It pulls them into your speech.

Tell stories that your audience can see themselves in. Not stories about you being amazing, but stories about you being human. Share a struggle, a mistake, a moment of doubt. When people see themselves reflected in your story, they connect emotionally. Emotion is what makes people remember.

Create moments of interaction. Ask for a show of hands. Have people turn to a neighbour and discuss something for 30 seconds. If it's a small group, ask direct questions. These moments break up the monotony and keep brains engaged. Attention naturally dips after 10-15 minutes, so interaction refreshes it.

Use concrete examples and specifics. Don't say "some people struggle with public speaking." Say "Sarah, a software engineer from Melbourne, told me she used to get so nervous before presentations that she'd feel physically sick." Specifics are memorable. Generalities are forgettable.

Match your energy to your message. If you're talking about something exciting, be excited. If you're being serious, be serious. Mismatched energy confuses people. Your genuine enthusiasm is contagious.14:T7ba,Questions are gifts, not attacks. When someone asks a question, they're showing they were listening and engaged. Even difficult questions are opportunities to clarify, demonstrate expertise, or show humility. The key is not panicking when you don't know the answer.

First, listen fully to the question. Don't interrupt or start formulating your answer before they finish. Nod to show you're listening. If the question is unclear, ask for clarification: "Just to make sure I understand, are you asking about...?" This buys you thinking time and ensures you answer the right question.

Repeat or rephrase the question before answering. This serves multiple purposes: it confirms you understood correctly, it gives you a moment to think, and it ensures everyone in the room heard the question. Say: "That's a great question. You're asking how this applies to remote teams, right?"

If you don't know the answer, say so. Honesty is far more credible than making something up. You could say: "That's a brilliant question and I don't have the answer off the top of my head. Let me look into that and get back to you." Then actually follow up. This builds trust.

If you know the answer, give a concise response. Don't ramble. Answer the question directly, then stop. Nervous speakers often keep talking to fill silence, which dilutes their message. One clear sentence is better than three rambling ones.

For hostile or tricky questions, stay calm. Take a breath. Don't get defensive. Acknowledge the concern: "I understand why you'd ask that." Then answer thoughtfully. If someone's being genuinely difficult, you can say: "That's an important point. Let's discuss it after so we can stay on schedule."

Practice thinking on your feet by doing impromptu speaking exercises. Toastmasters has "Table Topics" where you speak for 1-2 minutes on random subjects. This trains your brain to access knowledge quickly without preparation. The more you do it, the more comfortable you become.15:T7bd,Slides are like seasoning in cooking. A little enhances the dish. Too much ruins it. Your slides should support your speech, not replace it. If someone could understand your entire message just by reading your slides, you're not needed. You're there to add depth, context, and human connection that slides can't provide.

Start with the rule of six: no more than six lines of text per slide, no more than six words per line. This forces you to be concise. If you're tempted to put a paragraph on a slide, that's a sign you're trying to do too much. Break it into multiple slides or just speak it.

Use visuals, not text. A powerful image communicates faster than words. If you're talking about climate change, show a striking photograph rather than listing statistics. If you're explaining a process, use a diagram. Visuals stick in memory far better than bullet points.

Choose a clean, simple design. Avoid busy backgrounds, excessive animations, or clashing colours. Your audience should focus on your message, not be distracted by design choices. Stick to 2-3 colours maximum. Use readable fonts (sans-serif like Arial or Helvetica work well). Make text large enough that someone in the back row can read it.

One idea per slide. Don't cram multiple concepts onto one slide. Let each slide breathe. This also forces you to slow down and give each idea proper attention.

Use data visualisation wisely. A well-designed chart can communicate complex information quickly. A poorly designed one confuses people. Keep charts simple. Avoid 3D effects and unnecessary decorations. Label axes clearly.

Practise with your slides beforehand. Know which slide comes next. Use presenter notes if needed, but don't read directly from slides. Glance at them occasionally, but keep your focus on the audience.

Remember: you are the presentation. Slides are supporting actors. If your slides disappeared, could you still deliver a compelling talk?
