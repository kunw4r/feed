4:T61a,Psychology is the scientific study of behaviour and the mind. Think of it like studying the instruction manual for how humans think, feel, and act. It's not just about therapy or mental illness—it's about understanding why you prefer coffee over tea, why you laugh at jokes, and how you make decisions.

Your brain is the control centre. It contains roughly 86 billion neurons (brain cells) that communicate with each other through electrical and chemical signals. Imagine neurons as tiny post offices passing messages along a vast network. When you learn something new, these connections strengthen. When you stop using a skill, those connections weaken.

The brain has different regions that do different jobs. The prefrontal cortex (behind your forehead) handles planning and decision-making. The amygdala (deep in your brain) processes emotions and fear. The hippocampus stores memories. They all work together like departments in a company.

Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that help neurons communicate. Dopamine makes you feel motivated and rewarded. Serotonin affects mood and happiness. Cortisol is your stress hormone. When these chemicals are balanced, you feel good. When they're out of balance, you might feel anxious or depressed.

Psychology uses the scientific method to study behaviour. Researchers form hypotheses, run experiments, collect data, and draw conclusions. This means psychology isn't just opinion—it's evidence-based. Understanding psychology helps you recognise patterns in your own behaviour and make better choices.5:T674,Memory isn't like a video recorder that captures everything perfectly. It's more like a sketch artist—your brain captures the main details and fills in the rest based on what makes sense. This is why eyewitness accounts are often unreliable, and why you remember the gist of a conversation but not exact words.

There are three stages of memory. First, sensory memory holds information for just a few seconds—like seeing a flash of light. Second, short-term (or working) memory holds about 7 items for roughly 30 seconds. Try remembering a phone number you just heard. Finally, long-term memory stores information for days, years, or a lifetime. Your childhood memories and knowledge of how to ride a bike live here.

To move information from short-term to long-term memory, you need to rehearse it. Repetition works, but spacing it out works better. Studying for 30 minutes daily beats cramming for 3 hours the night before. This is called spaced repetition, and it's why flashcard apps are effective.

You also forget on purpose. Your brain prunes away unused information to make room for what matters. If you don't use a skill, the neural connections weaken. This is called the "use it or lose it" principle. That's why you forget languages you haven't spoken in years.

Context matters too. You remember things better when you learn them in the same environment where you'll use them. Studying in the library helps if you'll take the exam there. Emotional memories stick around longer because emotions trigger stronger neural connections. You'll remember where you were during a shocking news event, but forget what you had for lunch yesterday.6:T73f,Your brain is lazy. It takes shortcuts to save energy, and these shortcuts often lead you astray. These mental shortcuts are called cognitive biases, and they're universal—everyone has them. Understanding them helps you make better decisions.

Confirmation bias is when you seek out information that confirms what you already believe and ignore information that contradicts it. If you think coffee is bad for you, you'll remember news articles about coffee's health risks and forget the ones saying it's safe. Your brain is protecting your existing belief.

The availability heuristic makes you overestimate how likely something is based on how easily you remember examples. After watching a plane crash documentary, you might think flying is dangerous, even though car accidents are far more common. Dramatic events stick in memory, so your brain thinks they're more frequent.

Anchor bias happens when the first number you hear influences your judgment. If a shop shows a crossed-out price of £100 next to a £50 price tag, you think you're getting a bargain—even if £50 is the normal price. That first number anchors your thinking.

The sunk cost fallacy makes you continue investing in something because you've already invested time or money. You stay in a bad relationship because you've been together for years, or watch a bad film because you've already watched half of it. Your brain tries to justify past decisions rather than make the best future choice.

Recency bias makes you overweight recent events. After a bad day at work, you might think your job is terrible, forgetting the good days last week. Your brain prioritises what just happened.

These biases aren't character flaws—they're features of how your brain evolved to make quick decisions with limited information. Recognising them is the first step to thinking more clearly.7:T79b,Motivation isn't something you're born with—it's something your brain creates based on rewards and needs. Understanding what drives you helps you change habits and achieve goals.

There are two types of motivation. Intrinsic motivation comes from within—you do something because it's interesting, meaningful, or enjoyable. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside rewards or punishments. You exercise because you enjoy it (intrinsic) or because you want to look good for an event (extrinsic). Interestingly, research shows intrinsic motivation is more sustainable. If you exercise because you love it, you'll stick with it longer than if you only do it for external rewards.

Habits form through a three-part loop: cue, routine, reward. A cue triggers the behaviour (your alarm goes off), you perform the routine (you brush your teeth), and you get a reward (your mouth feels fresh). Your brain learns this loop and eventually performs it automatically, without conscious thought. This is why habits are powerful—they require almost no willpower once formed.

To build a new habit, make the cue obvious, the routine easy, and the reward immediate. Want to exercise more? Put your gym clothes by your bed (obvious cue), start with just 10 minutes (easy routine), and enjoy a smoothie afterwards (immediate reward). Small changes compound over time.

To break a bad habit, keep the cue and reward but change the routine. If you eat biscuits when stressed (cue: stress, routine: eat biscuits, reward: comfort), replace the routine with something else that provides comfort—like tea or a walk. The cue and reward stay the same, but the behaviour changes.

Willpower is limited. Your brain uses glucose to maintain self-control, and it depletes throughout the day. This is why you make poor food choices at night after a long day. Design your environment to make good choices easier. If unhealthy snacks aren't in your house, you can't eat them.8:T7f9,Emotional intelligence (EI) is your ability to recognise, understand, and manage emotions—both your own and others'. It's not about being happy all the time. It's about understanding what your emotions are telling you and responding wisely.

Emotions serve a purpose. Fear alerts you to danger. Anger signals that a boundary has been crossed. Sadness helps you process loss. Disgust protects you from harmful things. Your emotions are data—they contain important information about what matters to you and what you need.

Emotional intelligence has four components. First, self-awareness means recognising your emotions as they happen. Instead of snapping at someone and wondering why later, you notice "I'm feeling frustrated right now." Second, self-management means controlling your response to emotions. You feel angry, but you don't yell. Third, social awareness means recognising emotions in others—reading facial expressions, tone of voice, and body language. Finally, relationship management means using emotional understanding to navigate social situations effectively.

Emotional regulation is a key skill. When you're upset, your amygdala (emotional brain) takes over and your prefrontal cortex (rational brain) goes offline. This is why you can't think straight when emotional. Techniques like deep breathing, pausing before responding, and naming your emotion help calm your amygdala and restore rational thinking. Simply labelling an emotion—"I'm anxious"—reduces its intensity.

Empathy is understanding what someone else feels. It's not the same as sympathy (feeling sorry for someone). Empathy means stepping into their shoes and understanding their perspective. This skill is crucial for relationships, leadership, and conflict resolution.

People with high emotional intelligence tend to have better relationships, perform better at work, and have better mental health. The good news is that emotional intelligence can be developed. It's not fixed—it's a skill you can practise and improve throughout your life.9:T86b,You behave differently alone than you do in a group. Social psychology studies how the presence of others influences your thoughts, feelings, and behaviour. Understanding this helps you recognise when you're being influenced and make more authentic choices.

Social facilitation is when your performance improves on simple tasks but worsens on complex tasks when others are watching. You'll perform better at a familiar sport in front of a crowd, but struggle with a new skill. Your brain gets aroused by an audience, which helps with well-practised tasks but hurts with difficult ones.

Deindividuation happens when you lose your sense of individual identity in a group. You might do things in a crowd that you'd never do alone—like cheering aggressively at a football match or joining in group mockery. Your individual responsibility dissolves into the group, and social norms weaken. This is why mobs can behave badly.

Conformity is the tendency to match the behaviour and beliefs of the group. In famous experiments, people gave obviously wrong answers just because everyone else did. We're wired to fit in—it was a survival advantage in our evolutionary past. But conformity can lead to poor decisions when groups suppress dissenting opinions.

Groupthink occurs when a group prioritises harmony and agreement over critical thinking. Teams make worse decisions because members don't voice concerns or challenge ideas. This happens in boardrooms, governments, and sports teams. Diverse perspectives and encouraging dissent prevent groupthink.

Obedience to authority is another powerful influence. In the Milgram experiments, ordinary people administered painful electric shocks to others simply because an authority figure told them to. We're surprisingly willing to follow orders, even when they conflict with our values.

The good news is awareness helps. When you recognise these influences, you can resist them. Ask yourself: Am I conforming because I genuinely agree, or because I want to fit in? Am I following orders because they're right, or because of the uniform?
