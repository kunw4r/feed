3:T6e8,## Historical Context
Europe's far-right resurgence echoes the 1930s economic anxiety but operates in a modern context. Post-2008 financial crisis, austerity policies hit working-class voters hard. The 2015 refugee crisis (over 1 million arrivals) became a flashpoint. Unlike the 1930s, today's movements use social media and claim to be democratic, not explicitly fascist, making them harder to delegitimise.

## Multiple Perspectives
**Far-right supporters argue:** Immigration strains housing, jobs, and public services. EU rules prioritise corporate interests over workers. National sovereignty matters more than Brussels bureaucracy. They claim mainstream parties ignore these concerns.

**EU establishment view:** Far-right parties exploit legitimate concerns with false scapegoats. Immigration is economically net-positive long-term. EU integration prevents war and poverty. Populism oversimplifies complex problems.

**Centrist worry:** If far-right parties enter government, they could dismantle democratic norms (as happened in Hungary under Orbán), making it harder to remove them later.

## Future Implications
If Dutch far-right joins government, it sets a precedent. Other EU countries may follow. This could fracture EU unity on climate action, defence spending (especially NATO), and tech regulation. It might also embolden similar movements in Australia and other democracies.

## What to Watch
- Coalition negotiations: Will mainstream parties compromise with far-right or form alternative coalitions?
- Hungary and Poland: Will they align with Dutch far-right to block EU reforms?
- France 2027: Will Marine Le Pen's National Rally win the presidency?
