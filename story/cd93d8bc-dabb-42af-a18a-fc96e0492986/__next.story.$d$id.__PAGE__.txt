3:T674,The World Health Organisation (WHO) announced in February 2026 that its Pandemic Preparedness Fund—created after COVID-19 to prevent future global health emergencies—is running out of money because wealthy nations are cutting their contributions. Here's the structure: The WHO is funded in two ways. First, member countries pay 'assessed contributions' (mandatory payments based on their wealth and population—Australia pays about $20 million annually). Second, countries make 'voluntary contributions' to specific programs. The Pandemic Preparedness Fund relies entirely on voluntary contributions, and these have dropped 40% since 2023 as countries faced economic pressures and domestic political pressure to spend money at home instead of internationally.

Why this matters: The fund was supposed to help developing nations build early warning systems for disease outbreaks, train healthcare workers, and stockpile vaccines and treatments. Without it, poorer countries can't detect new pandemics quickly, meaning diseases spread further before the world even knows about them. This affects everyone—a new pandemic starting in a country without surveillance systems could reach Australia within weeks. The WHO itself is also politically weakened. When countries don't fund it, they ignore its recommendations. This is already happening: several nations rejected WHO guidance on avian flu protocols in 2025.

The WHO was founded in 1948 as a UN agency to coordinate global health. It's governed by 194 member states through the World Health Assembly (WHA), which meets annually. The organisation has no enforcement power—it can only recommend and coordinate. COVID-19 exposed massive gaps in pandemic preparedness: wealthy nations hoarded vaccines, poor nations had no testing capacity, and there was no global early warning system. The Pandemic Preparedness Fund was created in 2022 as a response, with an initial target of $30 billion over 10 years.

## Multiple Perspectives
**Wealthy nations' view**: They've spent trillions on COVID-19 response and domestic healthcare. They argue developing nations should fund their own health systems rather than relying on charity. Some also question whether the WHO is effective enough to deserve funding.

**Developing nations' view**: They argue wealthy nations created the problem by not sharing vaccines equitably during COVID-19. They say rich countries should fund global health security as a shared responsibility, not charity.

**Public health experts**: They warn that underfunded pandemic preparedness is a false economy—spending $1 on prevention costs far less than spending $1 trillion on pandemic response.

**Pharmaceutical companies**: They're lobbying for guaranteed government funding for vaccine development, arguing that market forces alone won't incentivise pandemic preparedness.

## Future Implications
If pandemic preparedness funding collapses, the next major disease outbreak will likely spread faster and kill more people than COVID-19. This could trigger a political backlash against the WHO and international health cooperation generally. Alternatively, a major outbreak might force countries to suddenly increase funding—but by then it's too late.
