New analysis shows trade barriers between the UK and EU cost British businesses approximately £40 billion annually in lost exports, higher import costs, and administrative burdens. Negotiations over post-Brexit trade arrangements have stalled, leaving key issues unresolved: Northern Ireland's trade status, financial services access, and data sharing agreements. The UK's economic growth rate (1.2% annually) lags behind comparable nations like Germany (2.1%), and the Office for Budget Responsibility warns Brexit will permanently reduce UK GDP by 4% compared to staying in the EU.

Why this matters: Brexit was sold as a way to reduce regulation and boost the economy. Instead, British businesses face more paperwork, tariffs, and delays when trading with Europe—their largest market. Small companies especially struggle with customs documentation and compliance costs. The UK government is attempting to negotiate "closer alignment" with EU rules to reduce friction, but the EU demands the UK accept EU regulations without having a say in making them. This creates a political dilemma: accepting EU rules undermines the "sovereignty" argument for Brexit, but rejecting them keeps trade barriers high.

For Australia, this matters because the UK is a major trading partner and investment source. A weaker UK economy means less demand for Australian goods and potentially less investment in Australian companies.
