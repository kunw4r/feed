Australia's defence spending has fluctuated significantly over the past 70 years, reflecting changing threat perceptions and economic conditions. During the Cold War (1950s-1980s), defence spending regularly exceeded 3% of GDP as Australia maintained military readiness against Soviet expansion and regional conflicts. After the Cold War ended, spending declined to around 1.5-1.8% of GDP during the 1990s and 2000s. The 2008 global financial crisis and subsequent economic challenges kept spending relatively flat. However, the rise of China as a military power, particularly after 2010, prompted gradual increases in defence spending. The 2026 increase to 2.3% represents a significant acceleration of this trend and signals Australia's determination to maintain military capabilities in a more contested regional environment.

## Multiple Perspectives
Defence and security officials argue that increased spending is essential for national security, pointing to China's military modernisation, Russia's aggression in Ukraine, and North Korea's weapons development. They contend that defence spending is an investment in economic security, as military instability would disrupt trade worth hundreds of billions of dollars annually. Economists note that defence spending has multiplier effects, supporting manufacturing jobs, technology development, and regional economies. However, critics argue that the spending increase reflects Cold War thinking inappropriate for the modern era. Social advocates contend that the money would address pressing domestic needs—healthcare, education, mental health services, and climate change adaptation. Some international relations experts worry that increased defence spending could trigger an arms race in the region, making conflict more likely rather than less. Others argue that Australia's spending increase is modest compared to regional powers like China and India, which spend 1.7% and 2.4% of GDP respectively.

## Future Implications
If Australia maintains defence spending at 2.3% of GDP, it will become one of the world's top 15 military spenders by absolute dollars, significantly enhancing military capabilities. This could allow Australia to develop indigenous defence capabilities (such as submarines and missiles) rather than relying entirely on imports. However, sustained high spending could crowd out other government investments and limit fiscal flexibility during economic downturns. If other regional nations respond with their own spending increases, an arms race could develop, increasing regional tensions. Alternatively, if Australia's spending increase is matched by diplomatic efforts to reduce tensions, it could contribute to regional stability.
