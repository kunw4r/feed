This created opportunities for money laundering, terrorism financing, and tax evasion. The new framework, finalised this week, requires all crypto exchanges to verify customer identity (like banks do), report suspicious transactions, and comply with local tax laws. Australia's ASIC and the ATO will now have powers to regulate crypto like they do traditional finance. Here's why this matters: crypto was supposed to be decentralised and free from government control, but governments realised this made them vulnerable to criminals and terrorists. The new rules will make crypto safer for legitimate users but less anonymous. Bitcoin and Ethereum prices have already fallen 8-12% as traders worry about reduced anonymity and increased compliance costs. Bitcoin was created in 2009 as a response to the 2008 financial crisis, designed to be decentralised and free from government control. For over a decade, crypto operated in regulatory grey zones. The 2022 collapse of FTX (a major crypto exchange) exposed massive fraud and sparked regulatory urgency. El Salvador's adoption of Bitcoin as legal tender (2021) showed governments were experimenting with crypto. China banned crypto trading in 2021, forcing exchanges to move offshore. The US, EU, and now G20 have progressively tightened rules.

## Multiple Perspectives
**Crypto advocates** argue regulation kills innovation and defeats crypto's purpose of decentralisation. **Financial regulators** say crypto's anonymity enables crime and must be controlled. **Developing nations** see crypto as a way to bypass corrupt banking systems and access global finance. **Law enforcement** supports regulation to combat money laundering and terrorism financing. **Tax authorities** want to prevent wealthy individuals hiding assets in crypto.

## Future Implications
Crypto will likely become a regulated asset class, similar to stocks and bonds. This could reduce volatility and fraud, attracting institutional investors. However, it may also reduce crypto's appeal to those seeking anonymity. Decentralised finance (DeFi) platforms that operate without intermediaries may face pressure or bans. Developing nations may resist regulation if it limits financial inclusion.
