3:T517,Sri Lanka has defaulted on its international bonds again, and Australian banks and investment funds are losing money. This is a good moment to understand what bonds actually are and why they matter. A bond is essentially a loan: when a government or company needs money, they issue bonds—basically IOUs—to investors. You lend them money, they promise to pay you interest every year, and return your principal (original money) at the end. Bonds are considered safer than stocks because they're a guaranteed payment, not a share of profits. However, if the borrower can't pay—like Sri Lanka right now—you lose your money. Sri Lanka borrowed heavily in US dollars to fund development projects, but when tourism collapsed during COVID and global interest rates rose, they couldn't afford repayments. They've now defaulted multiple times. Australian pension funds and banks own Sri Lankan bonds worth hundreds of millions of dollars. When Sri Lanka doesn't pay, these funds lose money, which affects Australian retirees and bank shareholders. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) is negotiating a restructuring deal where bondholders might recover 30-50 cents per dollar owed. Bonds have been used for centuries—governments issued bonds to finance wars and infrastructure. The modern bond market exploded after World War II. Developing nations increasingly borrowed in foreign currencies (especially US dollars) from the 1980s onward, making them vulnerable to exchange rate shocks. Sri Lanka's debt crisis echoes Argentina (2001), Greece (2010), and Lebanon (2019)—all countries that borrowed heavily in foreign currency and faced default.

## Multiple Perspectives
**Creditors** (including Australian funds) argue Sri Lanka should prioritise repayment and implement IMF reforms. **Sri Lankan citizens** are suffering austerity measures and inflation as the government cuts spending to service debt. **Developing nation advocates** argue the global financial system is rigged—rich countries can borrow cheaply, poor countries face predatory interest rates. **IMF officials** say restructuring is necessary to prevent total collapse.

## Future Implications
More developing nations may default as global interest rates remain elevated. This could trigger a 'lost decade' of slow growth in the Global South. Australia's exposure to emerging market debt may force super funds to diversify away from these assets, reducing capital flows to developing nations.
