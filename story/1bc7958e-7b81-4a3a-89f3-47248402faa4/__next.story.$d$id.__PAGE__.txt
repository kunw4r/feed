3:T8b0,## Historical Context
Australia's housing crisis has been building for 20 years. Population growth (immigration, natural increase) outpaced housing construction. Between 2001-2021, Australia's population grew 35% but housing stock grew only 25%. By 2026, the shortage is estimated at 250,000-500,000 homes. Rents have surged because supply is tight; prices have risen because interest rates were low (2020-2022) and investors competed with owner-occupiers. The 2023-2024 rental crisis saw young Australians priced out of major cities, sparking political pressure on both major parties.

## Multiple Perspectives
**Labor's argument**: Only government can solve this via direct investment and planning reform. Private developers won't build affordable housing without incentives. Fast-track approvals remove red tape.

**Coalition's argument**: Government spending is inflationary and inefficient. Remove planning restrictions, reduce taxes on developers, let markets work. Private sector builds faster and cheaper.

**Economists**: Both plans face the same constraint—construction labour shortage. Australia lacks 100,000+ construction workers. Even with $50bn, if you can't find builders, homes won't be built. Supply-side reforms (immigration of skilled tradespeople) matter more than money.

**Renters/first-time buyers**: Both plans are too slow. Even 1.2 million homes over 10 years = 120,000/year. Current shortage is 250,000+. Relief won't come before 2030.

## Future Implications
If either plan succeeds partially, housing supply growth could outpace demand by 2028-2030, easing rent and price growth. This would reduce inflation, allowing the RBA to cut rates faster. Politically, whichever party wins the 2026 election will own the housing crisis—if supply doesn't improve, they'll face backlash in 2028-2030.

## What to Watch
- Construction starts data (monthly): Are developers actually building, or waiting for policy clarity?
- Migration numbers: If immigration slows, housing demand falls, easing pressure
- Rent growth rates: If rents stabilise, political pressure eases; if they keep rising, both parties lose credibility
