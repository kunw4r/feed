3:T665,Representatives from the European Union and United States met in Geneva on 16 February 2026 to negotiate global AI safety standards, but fundamental disagreements emerged about how strictly to regulate artificial intelligence development. The EU advocated for mandatory safety testing before companies release new AI models, similar to pharmaceutical approval processes, while the US delegation argued that such requirements would stifle innovation and hand competitive advantage to China. The debate centred on who should bear responsibility when AI systems cause harm—the companies that built them, the organisations that deployed them, or the users who operated them.

The EU's AI Act, which came into effect in 2024, requires companies to assess risks and maintain detailed documentation of their systems. The US has resisted comparable federal legislation, instead relying on industry self-regulation and sector-specific rules. China, meanwhile, has implemented strict content controls on AI systems while encouraging rapid development of domestic AI capabilities. These divergent approaches create practical problems for companies operating globally, as they must maintain different versions of their systems for different regions.

Both sides acknowledged the stakes are enormous, with AI potentially affecting everything from job markets to national security. However, they could not agree on enforcement mechanisms or how to handle AI systems developed by companies in countries that refuse to adopt safety standards. The summit ended without binding agreements, though negotiators scheduled follow-up meetings for April 2026.4:T7ee,## Historical Context
AI regulation is entirely new territory for governments. The EU's AI Act represents the first comprehensive attempt to regulate AI at a continental scale, categorising systems by risk level and imposing obligations on developers. The US has historically favoured lighter regulation, allowing companies to self-regulate while government focuses on specific harms (like discrimination in hiring). This reflects deeper philosophical differences: the EU emphasises precaution and consumer protection, while the US emphasises market competition and innovation.

## Multiple Perspectives
Tech companies generally oppose strict pre-release testing, arguing it will slow development and increase costs, potentially benefiting only large corporations that can afford compliance. Safety advocates counter that waiting for harm to occur before regulating is irresponsible when AI systems affect millions of people. Developing nations worry that strict regulations will entrench advantages of wealthy countries that can afford compliance costs. Some researchers argue that no regulatory framework can keep pace with AI development, making any regulation obsolete within months.

## Future Implications
If the EU and US cannot align on AI standards, we may see a fragmented global AI landscape where companies maintain different systems for different regions, increasing costs and complexity. This could accelerate a "splinternet" where different countries develop incompatible AI ecosystems. Alternatively, one region's standards may become dominant globally if that region has sufficient market power or technological leadership.
