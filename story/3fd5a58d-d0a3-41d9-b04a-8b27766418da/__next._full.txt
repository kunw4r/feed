10:T6bb,## Historical Context
The EU has a history of tough tech regulation: GDPR (2018) set privacy standards now copied globally. The AI Act follows this pattern. It emerged from concerns about algorithmic bias (e.g., Amazon's hiring AI discriminated against women), deepfakes, and surveillance. Unlike the US (which favours light regulation) and China (which uses AI for state control), the EU chose a middle path: innovation with guardrails.

## Multiple Perspectives
**EU regulators argue:** AI systems already cause real harm—biased loan algorithms deny mortgages to minorities, facial recognition misidentifies people of colour. Regulation prevents this. Europe's strength is values, not speed; we should lead on ethics, not just speed-to-market.

**Tech companies claim:** Rules are vague and costly. Compliance requires hiring armies of lawyers and engineers. This slows innovation and pushes startups to relocate. The US and China will leapfrog Europe.

**Developing countries worry:** They can't afford compliance costs, so they'll be locked out of EU markets. This widens the tech gap.

## Future Implications
If the AI Act succeeds, it becomes the global standard (like GDPR). This could slow AI development globally but reduce harms. If it fails (too costly, too slow), companies will lobby for rollback, and the EU's regulatory credibility weakens.

## What to Watch
- First enforcement actions: Which companies will be fined first, and for what violations?
- US response: Will the US impose counter-regulations or pressure the EU to weaken rules?
- China's AI advantage: Will Chinese companies dominate because they face no restrictions?
