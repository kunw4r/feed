10:T9fa,## Historical Context
Australia's economy is heavily dependent on commodity exports, particularly to China. Since 2000, China's industrialisation created massive demand for iron ore (for steel), coal (for energy), and lithium (for batteries). Australian miners became wealthy; the government collected royalties and taxes. By 2026, China accounts for ~30% of Australian exports by value. The US-China trade war began in 2018 under Trump, paused under Biden, and has reignited in 2026 as Trump returns to office. The core issue: the US views China as a strategic competitor in technology and military capability. Tariffs are a weapon to slow China's economy and force it to buy US goods instead. But tariffs also hurt US allies like Australia, whose exports depend on Chinese demand.

## Multiple Perspectives
**US perspective**: China is a strategic threat. Tariffs force China to negotiate on technology theft, military spending, and Taiwan. Short-term pain for long-term security.

**Chinese perspective**: US tariffs are economic warfare. China will retaliate and accelerate its own technology development to reduce US dependence. Long-term, China will shift to other suppliers (Australia, Indonesia, Brazil).

**Australian miners**: We're caught in the middle. We depend on Chinese demand but are US allies. Tariffs reduce Chinese demand, hurting our profits and workers. We need government support (subsidies, tax breaks) to survive.

**Australian government**: We're balancing US alliance with economic dependence on China. We can't afford to lose either relationship. We're lobbying both sides to negotiate.

## Future Implications
If the trade war escalates, iron ore could fall to $80-100/tonne (from current $120+), costing Australia $20-30bn/year in lost export revenue. This would reduce government tax revenue, forcing spending cuts or tax rises. Mining job losses could reach 50,000+. Conversely, if the US and China negotiate (unlikely), tariffs could be rolled back, and prices could recover. The 2026 election will be fought partly on which party can better manage this shock.

## What to Watch
- Iron ore prices (daily): Spot prices indicate Chinese demand
- Chinese economic data (monthly): GDP, industrial production, property sector. Weak data = lower commodity demand
- US-China negotiations: Any talks suggest tariff rollback is possible
- Australian government response: Will it subsidise miners? Cut taxes? Invest in alternative industries?
